How CBAM works

CBAM, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, makes EU importers pay for the carbon in certain goods, at about the price EU producers pay under the EU's emissions trading system. It covers iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity.

Importers only reported emissions from October 2023 to December 2025. Money became due for goods imported from 1 January 2026, and the importer pays, so an Indian exporter never receives a CBAM bill. It feels the charge in the price a buyer will accept, and in the data the buyer asks for.

The dates that matter

  1. Oct 2023Importers start quarterly reporting. Nothing is paid yet.
  2. Oct 2025A simplification law sets a 50-tonne yearly threshold per importer.
  3. Jan 2026The financial stage begins. Imports from this date carry a CBAM cost.
  4. Jan 2026India and the EU conclude a trade deal. It gives no CBAM exemption.
  5. Jul 2026EU steel quotas replace the safeguard, with a 50% duty outside quota.
  6. Sep 2026The Commission expected the first verifier accreditations around now.
  7. Jan 2027The UK starts its own CBAM. Verification work on 2026 data can begin.
  8. Feb 2027Certificates go on sale, for 2026 and 2027 imports.
  9. Sep 2027First annual declaration and surrender, covering 2026 imports.
  10. 2028The default mark-up reaches its top rate of 30%. Operators can share verified data through the registry.
  11. 2034Free allocation for CBAM sectors reaches zero under current law. A 2026 proposal would slow this.

Six things people get wrong

Only 2.5% of the bill is due in 2026

That holds at the EU benchmark. Free allocation is subtracted as a fixed amount per tonne, so any emissions above the benchmark are charged in full. The Commission's Q&A sets out the sum at 3.7 and 3.8, and the fastener distributors' guide prints the same formula.

The exporter pays

The authorised declarant in the EU pays. The exporter's exposure is commercial: a buyer compares landed costs across origins, so the extra cost above a rival comes out of the exporter's price.

India's carbon market will cancel it out

CBAM allows a deduction for a carbon price actually paid. As of October 2026 no third country's price had been recognised, and India's market had recorded no certificate trade.

Electricity bills matter for steel

For iron, steel and aluminium goods the definitive period counts direct emissions and precursors only. Indirect emissions from electricity are left out.

Old stock escapes

Goods made in 2025 and imported in 2026 still fall under CBAM. The fastener distributors note one useful detail: goods imported in 2027 can use the lower 2026 default if the buyer can show they were made before 2027.

The rules on fasteners are settled

They are not. The fastener distributors say the Commission confirmed that making the fastener itself sits outside the boundary, and that wire preparation remains uncertain. The implementing regulation's wording on forging reads differently to me. This case keeps the plant's emissions in, which is the cautious choice, and the tool lets you set them to zero.

Terms

Embedded emissions
The CO₂e released making a good, including the steel or aluminium that went into it.
Default value
A Commission figure per product and country, used when no verified data exists.
Mark-up
A surcharge on default values: 10% in 2026, 20% in 2027, 30% from 2028.
Benchmark
A per-tonne emissions figure drawn from the EU's free allocation rules. Column A covers one process step and Column B includes the precursors.
CBAM factor
The share of free allocation EU producers still keep: 97.5% in 2026, falling to zero in 2034.
Free allocation adjustment
The amount taken off the bill: CBAM factor times benchmark, per tonne.
Certificate
One tonne of CO₂e, priced in line with the EU carbon market.
Precursor
A CBAM good used to make another, such as wire rod used to make bolts.
Verifier
An EU-accredited auditor. Actual emissions count only after verification.
CN code
The EU's product code. CN 7318 15 covers threaded screws and bolts.
