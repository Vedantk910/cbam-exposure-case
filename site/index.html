India's CBAM bill in 2026

The common shorthand says only 2.5% of embedded emissions is payable in 2026. That holds only at the EU benchmark. India's default value for bolts sits times above it, and everything above the benchmark is charged in full from the first day.

A strategy case by Vedant Konde, built on official EU data to October 2026.

The answer

A 600-tonne-a-year Indian fastener maker asks what to do about CBAM before 2034.

Buy verified rod from your current mill and verify your own plant. Don't wait, don't buy green steel yet, and don't leave the EU.

Five positions on one yardstick

Why

The bill is real now. On defaults a tonne of bolts carries in 2027, of what EU buyers pay. Across India's 2025 trade that is a year, against on the shorthand.

The money is in the rod, not the furnace. Verifying your own plant saves . Adding verified rod saves . of the prize comes from a document your mill holds.

Green steel loses for now. Free allocation falls with the cleaner route, which takes back of the gain in 2027. At a /t premium it needs carbon at /t in 2034, or a current mill above tCO₂e/t.

What to do first

  1. Ask your rod mill for its CBAM verification report on 2026 production. A verified figure below the marked-up default of tCO₂e/t beats the default from 2028.
  2. Line up a verifier now. The Commission expects first verification work in early 2027, the first visit must be physical, and the trigger is only about tonnes a year.
  3. Write the saving into your EU contracts. The importer pays CBAM and you pay for verification, so the split has to be agreed, not assumed.
  4. Buy as a cluster. One small exporter has little pull on a mill. A cluster's rod order has more, and it can share one verifier.

How I built it

This is the third case in a set: the first asks whether to acquire, the second whether to enter, and this one what to do when a regulator redraws your cost curve. I gave it the same seven gates. Each gate has an artefact in the repository, so anyone can check the work.

  1. Scope. One decision for one exporter, as five positions that cover every move.
  2. Build. Official workbooks and Eurostat into tagged facts, then a model of four small modules.
  3. Test. tests. The goldens were worked by hand from the formula, not read back from the code.
  4. Verify. One command regenerates every number. The report and this page read only that output.
  5. Stress. cases and four ablations, below.
  6. Document. Every fact tagged primary, official data, secondary or assumption.
  7. Hostile pass. The attacks a partner would make, answered with numbers, below.

What changed as the evidence came in

The plan's main finding was wrong. My first notes said a verified figure was worth about €1,400 in 2026, so verification could wait. They had taken the 2.5% at face value. The Commission's Q&A shows free allocation is subtracted as a benchmark, not applied as a percentage. That moved the 2026 figure by a factor of and reversed the advice.

My first yardstick was broken. I began with the combined cost to exporter and importer. That made leaving the EU look free. I rebuilt it around what the exporter absorbs after a rival's own CBAM bill, and the flip points stayed exactly the same.

My first regression failed its placebo. A fake start date in 2025 gave a t-statistic above 3, which meant the design was picking up something else. I had set each product's burden from 2025 prices, and those move with 2025 tonnes. Moving the base to 2023 took the placebo to and removed a 2025 surge that had looked like front-running.

Some sources would not open. EUR-Lex refuses automated access, so the equations come from the Commission's Q&A and benchmark workbooks, and the steel quota's product list is unread. Whether quotas touch fasteners stays marked as inferred.

The outcome

What it delivers

  • A corrected reading of the free allocation rule, sourced to the Commission.
  • A ranked answer with three triggers an exporter can act on.
  • A public tool that runs on any browser, with no login.
  • A repository where one command reproduces every number here.

What it does not show

  • No exporter, mill or verifier was interviewed. The exporter is fictional.
  • No Indian mill's verified figure is public, so 2.6 tCO₂e/t is an estimate and the biggest risk here.
  • Verifier fees are unpublished. The cost is a press figure at its upper end.
  • Unit values are customs averages, not contract prices.

What I would do next

Ask five mills whether they hold a verification report and at what intensity, then ask five exporters what their buyers have requested so far. Those two conversations test the two inputs that carry the most weight.
