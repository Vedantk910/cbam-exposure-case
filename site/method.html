How I worked on it

This is the third case in a set. The first asks whether to acquire, the second whether to enter a market, and this one what to do when a regulator redraws your cost curve. The record below shows how it moved, including the parts that went wrong.

Project log

  1. 27 Oct 2026The project starts as a plan for a software tool that turns an exporter's bills into a CBAM estimate. I set the scope to steel and aluminium goods. The working notes take one bolt maker through the implementing regulation.
  2. 28 Oct 2026The calculation spec is written, with 27 test cases set out before any code exists.
  3. 1 Nov 2026I change the goal from a software product to a strategy case with a working tool, in the format of my two earlier cases. A check against the Commission's Q&A overturns the plan's main finding, and the case is rebuilt on official data and published.
  4. 1 Nov 2026A review of the live site finds the tool drifting from the report through slider rounding. That gets fixed and locked with a test.
  5. 1 Nov 2026Second pass. Real plant data replaces my rod estimate, a trade body's worked examples become an outside test, and the EU buyer's side and the dependency map go in.

What changed as the evidence came in

The plan's main finding was wrong

The first notes put a verified figure at about €1,400 for 2026, so verification could wait. They had taken the 2.5% at face value. The Commission's Q&A shows free allocation is subtracted as a benchmark amount. That moved the 2026 figure by a factor of and reversed the advice.

The first yardstick was broken

I began with the combined cost to exporter and importer, which made leaving the EU look free. Rebuilding it around what the exporter absorbs after a rival's own CBAM bill fixed that, and the flip points stayed the same.

The first regression failed its placebo

A fake start date in 2025 gave a t-statistic above 3, so the design was picking up something else. Each product's burden had been set from 2025 prices, and those move with 2025 tonnes. Moving the base to 2023 took the placebo to and removed a 2025 surge that had looked like front-running.

One outside figure did not survive a source check

An early draft quoted a 15 to 22% estimate from a think tank. When the cited pages were read, the figure was not on any of them, so it came out.

The rod estimate was too kind

The first version assumed 2.6 tCO₂e/t. Ten named plants in India's draft schedule have a median of , so the base case moved to . The result got worse and the ranking held.

Some sources stayed closed

EUR-Lex refuses automated access, so the formula comes from the Commission's Q&A and benchmark files, and the steel quota's product list is unread. Whether quotas touch fasteners stays marked as inferred.

The outcome

What it delivers

  • A corrected reading of the free allocation rule, with a trade body's guide agreeing.
  • A ranked answer and the triggers that would change it.
  • A public tool for both sides of the trade, with no login.
  • A repository where one command reproduces every number here.

What it does not show

  • No exporter, mill or verifier was interviewed, and the exporter is fictional.
  • No Indian mill's verified CBAM figure is public. Plant numbers are draft baselines.
  • Verifier fees are unpublished. The cost here is a press figure at its upper end.
  • Unit values are customs averages, so they are not contract prices.
  • Carbon is held flat at the latest quarterly price, which is no forecast.

What I would do next

Ask five mills whether they hold a verification report and at what intensity, and ask five exporters what their buyers have requested so far. Those two conversations test the inputs carrying the most weight, and nothing on this site can stand in for them.
